# Housepoint

Point your phone at a house and see its story: when it was built, what it last sold for, and what it's valued at. It covers 26 states and DC, and big counties and cities in other states:

- **Property records and lot lines:** Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Jersey, Florida, North Carolina, Wisconsin, Washington, Nebraska, Minnesota, Washington DC, Maryland, New York, Vermont, Montana, Arkansas, Colorado, Wyoming, Utah, Alaska and Maine.
- **Big counties and cities:** Cook County (Chicago); Harris (Houston) and Tarrant (Fort Worth), Texas; Alameda and San Bernardino, California; Franklin (Columbus) and Hamilton (Cincinnati), Ohio; Nashville; Bismarck; the Portland area (Multnomah, Washington and Clackamas); Ada County (Boise); Oakland and Macomb, Michigan; Sedgwick County (Wichita); Atlanta; Maricopa County (Phoenix); Los Angeles County; Philadelphia; Allegheny County (Pittsburgh); Travis (Austin) and Bexar (San Antonio), Texas.
- **Lot lines and addresses only** (values are on the county's or town's site, linked where the state's map has the link): Indiana, New Hampshire, West Virginia, Nevada, Hawaii, Delaware and Virginia.
- **Everywhere else in the US, the basic card:** the building's use, footprint and height from the national building outlines (USA Structures), the nearest address on OpenStreetMap, flood zone, schools, neighborhood, safety, nearby places and a link to the record: the town's own assessor database in Rhode Island (its towns, not counties, keep the records), Tennessee's and Kansas's statewide property searches for the counties on them, and a search for the county's own record elsewhere.

**Live:** https://mjillson85.github.io/Housepoint/

## How it works
- Phone GPS + compass work out which property you're pointing at.
- Massachusetts: MassGIS statewide standardized assessor parcels.
- Connecticut: the Connecticut GIS Office's statewide CAMA and Parcel Layer (town assessor records, public domain). Near the Massachusetts line both states' records load together.
- New Jersey: the NJ Office of GIS statewide parcels joined to the state's MOD-IV tax list (assessed value, last sale, year built, and last year's actual tax bill). Towns assess at different shares of market value, so the app doesn't compare New Jersey values to sale prices.
- Florida: the Florida Geographic Information Office's statewide parcel layer (Dept. of Revenue tax roll from county property appraisers).
- North Carolina: NC OneMap's statewide parcels (each county's tax records in one layer: tax value, year built, the date of the last sale but not its price, and each county's own use codes, read by their descriptions). A few counties send no use codes (the card says "Use not listed") or no addresses (Guilford, Orange).
- Wisconsin: the State Cartographer's Office statewide parcels (every city, village and town's tax roll in one layer, public domain: assessed value, the state's market estimate, the class of property, and the actual tax bill from the 2025 roll; no year built or sales). Condo common records (no value, or Dane County's unit "CDM") aren't shown as units.
- Washington: WaTech's statewide parcels, the public copy without owners (each county's market value, the Department of Revenue's land use code, and a link to the county assessor's page; no year built or sales). King County lists a condo building as one property with the whole building's value, so its card says so.
- Nebraska: the State of Nebraska's statewide parcels (Office of the CIO; the public copy has no owner fields): assessed value, the state's property classification code, the last sale where the county sends it, and a link to the county assessor's page. Omaha's tax-increment financing lots come as two records on one outline and are added together; Lancaster County (Lincoln) sends no property type, so its zoning stands in.
- Minnesota: MnGeo's open statewide parcels (the roughly 60 counties that share their records openly; owner and mailing fields are on the layer but never requested): estimated market value, the county's tax figure, the county's own words (or numbers) for the property's class, year built, finished area and the last sale. Addresses come in parts and are joined; search asks for the house number and street name.
- Washington, DC: the tax lots ("owner polygons") from the DC Office of the Chief Technology Officer, with the Office of Tax and Revenue's records: assessed value, this tax year's tax, the last sale and its kind, and the use code. Each map load also joins, from the same service, the condo units on a condo lot (CONDORELATE and ITSPE) and every building's year built, size and rooms (the CAMA tables). Owner and mailing fields are never requested.
- Maryland: Maryland iMAP's parcel boundaries joined to the State Department of Assessments and Taxation's records: full market value, land use and building type, year built, size, the last sale (and whether it was at arm's length), and a link to the state's record. Condo units are points on the state's property data layer and are added to the condo's outline; street and water outlines are never requested.
- New York: the state's public tax parcels (Office of Information Technology Services, from the assessment rolls) for the counties that share them openly, New York City included: the state's property class (and New York City's eleven groups), year built, living area, bedrooms and baths. Outside New York City the full market value is shown where a town's figure carried over; otherwise, and in New York City, the assessment is shown and called a fraction of market value. No sales or tax. The layer's address search takes 10 to 20 seconds, so addresses are found by position.
- Vermont: town grand lists on VCGI's statewide parcels: listed value, land and buildings, the grand list category (home, seasonal camp, farm, commercial...). No street addresses on the records, so the card shows the nearest address on OpenStreetMap (Geoapify), and says so.
- Montana: the Department of Revenue's values on the Montana State Library's cadastral parcels: market value, land and buildings, the state's broad property type ("Improved Property" covers homes and businesses alike).
- Arkansas: county CAMA values on the Arkansas GIS Office's parcels (74 of 75 counties): appraised value, land and buildings, the two-letter parcel type.
- Cook County, Illinois (only): the Cook County Assessor's values on the county's parcel map, shown as market value (assessed value over the class's level: 10% homes, 25% businesses), year built from the building's age, building size, class, and a link to the assessor's page. A condo building is one lot with its units added up.
- Colorado: county records on the state's public parcels (OIT): actual value, last sale price and date, the county's property type words, a link to the county's page. Some counties (El Paso) share no values.
- Wyoming: county actual values on the state's 2026 parcels. No property type, so lots count as built when a building outline sits on them.
- Utah: county assessor records on UGRC's parcels, one service per county (the app asks the county around the spot): market value, year built, size, units, floors. Utah doesn't make sale prices public.
- Alaska: borough and city records on the state's parcels: assessed value, the local property type words. No street addresses (nearest one from OpenStreetMap, as in Vermont); some places (Juneau) share lot lines only.
- Maine: the towns' assessing records (values, the town's use words) joined to the Maine GeoLibrary's parcels by state id. Towns assess at their own share of market value.
- The big counties, each a region of its own inside a state that isn't otherwise covered (a search typed with "TX" looks in Harris and Tarrant):
  - Harris County (HCAD) and Tarrant County (TAD), Texas: market value, the state's property class (Harris), bedrooms, baths and year built (Tarrant). Texas doesn't make sale prices public.
  - Alameda and San Bernardino counties, California: Proposition 13 assessed values (often far below market), Alameda's use codes, San Bernardino's use words; San Bernardino has no street addresses (nearest one from OpenStreetMap).
  - Franklin and Hamilton counties, Ohio: the auditors' values, Ohio's land use classes, last sale; Hamilton's actual annual tax.
  - Nashville (Metro assessor), Bismarck (city assessor), the Portland area (Metro's tax lots with the three county assessors' real market value), Ada County, Idaho, Sedgwick County, Kansas (Wichita's layer), Atlanta (the city's tax parcels) and Maricopa County, Arizona (full cash value, last sale, a link to the assessor's page).
  - Los Angeles County: Proposition 13 assessed values on the assessor's parcels (GISNET), the use code's kind (house, two to four units, apartments, condo), the first building's year, size, bedrooms and baths, a link to the assessor's page. The layer doesn't answer an address search from a browser, so addresses are found by position.
  - Philadelphia: the Water Department's parcels joined to the Office of Property Assessment's records on the city's open data (Carto) by account number; a condo building's units come by its address and stack on its outline. Market value, last sale, row house / twin / detached, a link to the city's property page.
  - Allegheny County, Pennsylvania: the county's parcels joined to its assessments on the WPRDC (asked by JSONP, as the data center doesn't allow cross-site requests). Values are the 2012 base year's, so they're kept out of price rankings like California's.
  - Travis (TCAD) and Bexar (BCAD on the county's parcels), Texas: market value, the state property class, year built, a link to TCAD's page. Travis's condo units aren't on the county's map.
  - Oakland and Macomb counties, Michigan: Michigan assesses at half of market value, so the value shown is twice the assessed value; Macomb links each lot to its community's BS&A page.
- Indiana, New Hampshire, West Virginia, Nevada, Hawaii, Delaware and Virginia: the state's lot lines (and in Indiana, New Hampshire and West Virginia the address; Indiana adds the DLGF property class). The card says values are on the county's or town's site; Nevada and Hawaii link each lot to it. Nevada, Hawaii, Delaware and Virginia have no street addresses (nearest one from OpenStreetMap).
- The basic card: each building in the national outlines (FEMA and ORNL's USA Structures) is a "lot" of its own, with what the outlines say (residential or commercial and the kind, footprint, height, sometimes the address); the nearest address, town and county come from Geoapify (OpenStreetMap) by position. A region per state ("US_SC") is made when first needed; the state comes from the Census Bureau's cartographic state lines. A covered state's county that shares no records (in Minnesota, say) gets the basic card too.
- The compass correction is the World Magnetic Model (WMM2025) itself wherever no region has its own fit.
- Region outlines are the Census Bureau's state, county and city lines, about 400 m outside and cut back where a covered neighbour begins; near a line both sides' lots load.
- No tax estimate yet in the Tier 2 states or the newer regions (Hamilton County shows its actual tax).
- Owner names are never requested or shown, in any state. No accounts, ads or tracking; fonts are served from this site. See [privacy.html](privacy.html).
- Aerial photos: MassGIS 2025 aerial imagery in Massachusetts (15 cm, leaf-off), USGS NAIP elsewhere and as a fallback.
- Building outlines (to aim at roofs, not lawns): MassGIS Building Structures; FEMA/ORNL USA Structures in Connecticut, New Jersey, Florida, North Carolina, Wisconsin, Washington, Nebraska, Minnesota, DC, the Tier 2 states, Maryland, New York and the newer states and counties, with Florida's statewide footprints as a backup.
- Flood zone: FEMA National Flood Hazard Layer. Ground elevation: USGS Elevation Point Query Service.
- Map search: an address with a house number is looked up in the state parcel services first (the exact lot); then the U.S. Census Bureau geocoder (free, no key) finds the address, and the Census Bureau's TIGERweb map finds a town on its own. When a Geoapify key is set, Geoapify's address search leads for streets and towns and backs up the Census for house numbers (it handles typos). What you type is sent to these services.
- "Thinking of buying?" (on each card, loaded when opened): listing links (Zillow, a listings search), the nearest public schools and school district (NCES), Census tract figures next to the state's (American Community Survey), the local police department's crime rates from the FBI, and distances to groceries, transit, parks and more (OpenStreetMap places via Geoapify, with public Overpass servers as a backup). Free keys go in `KEYS` near the top of that section of index.html: a Census key (api.census.gov/data/key_signup.html), an api.data.gov key for the FBI, and a Geoapify key (free plan, 3,000 credits a day). Both are free rate-limit keys, not secrets.
- Estimated property tax: assessed value × the town's FY2026 residential rate (Massachusetts Division of Local Services); assessed value (70% of the town's appraisal) × the town's FY2026 mill rate (Connecticut OPM); last year's actual bill from the MOD-IV tax list (New Jersey); the county's taxable value × its average tax rate (Florida TaxWatch, from Dept. of Revenue data); or the tax value × the county's FY2026–27 rate plus the town's (NC Department of Revenue), with the county-only figure for houses outside town limits; last year's actual bill from the tax roll (Wisconsin); or the market value × the county's average levy rate for 2025 taxes (Washington Dept. of Revenue), a rough figure; or the assessed value × the county's 2025 average tax rate (Nebraska Dept. of Revenue), also rough; or the county's own tax figure for the property (Minnesota); this tax year's tax from the Office of Tax and Revenue (DC); or the full market value × the county's, town's and state's 2026–27 rates (Maryland, SDAT), rough. Except in New Jersey, Wisconsin, Minnesota and DC, it's an estimate, not the actual bill.

## Features
- **Point mode:** aim at a house and tap for its card. The app allows for your phone's GPS and compass being a little off: it weighs every house the aim could really be on and picks the most likely, out to about 260 ft. When two are close it says "or …" so you can choose, and the label doesn't flick between neighbors as the phone wobbles. Small tags float over the other houses in view that are near and wide enough on screen to tag reliably; tap one to open it. "Labels on/off" hides them. Distances are to the house's edge, rounded to 10 ft.
- **Card:** year built, value, last sale, price per square foot, estimated property tax, lot, flood zone and ground elevation, and a shareable image with a link that opens the same house on the map (`?h=lat,lon&k=key`).
- **Pointing:** the screen stays awake while you point (where the phone supports it).
- **Map:** color every lot by age, value or last sale year, with a legend.
- **Monthly cost:** in "Thinking of buying?", a mortgage calculator that starts from the card's own numbers (price, estimated tax, the state's average insurance) and Freddie Mac's latest 30-year rate. Type the asking price or your own rate; down payment and loan length are remembered.
- **Nearby:** walk or drive times to the closest grocery, pharmacy, park, train and more, a map with ¼, ½ and 1 mile rings (USGS base map), and filters for groceries, dining, parks, transit and health.
- **Property types:** each record is read from its state's use codes (homes, condos, apartments, stores, offices, land, exempt property), so a store gets floor area instead of living area, a condo's master record and a strip of land on the same lot aren't counted as units, and the home-buyer tools appear only for homes.
- **Save & compare:** star a house on its card; the Saved tab lines starred houses up side by side (value, last sale, tax, monthly cost, size, flood zone, nearest school and grocery). Saved houses stay on the phone.

## Try it
- Open the live link in Safari or Chrome on your phone, tap **Start pointing**, and allow camera, location and motion.
- Add `?demo=1` to the URL to explore a made-up neighborhood without going outside.
- Add `?health=1` to check the data services: each state's property records and the address lookups, how fast they answer, and whether one has moved.
- Add `?aimpow=0.25` to change how much a house's on-screen width is discounted when picking (0 favors big near houses, 1 treats every house alike; 0.25 tested best).
- Add `?debug=1` to see the compass, GPS and camera view readings. On an iPhone the first line also shows the compass accuracy (`acc ±N°`); the app warns when it's worse than ±30°.
- If the floating tags are all shifted to one side of the houses, that's compass error: `?decl=` sets the compass correction (about −14 in Massachusetts and Connecticut, −12 in New Jersey, −7 in Florida, −9 in Raleigh, −3 in Madison, +15 in Seattle, +2 in Omaha). If tags are spread too far apart or bunched together, `?fov=` sets how wide the camera view is, in degrees (about 34 on an iPhone held upright).

It's a single file (`index.html`), no build step.

## Tests
`tests/` holds the automated checks (headless Chrome; the public services are faked, so no network is needed):

    cd tests && npm install && npm test

They also run on GitHub after every push (`.github/workflows/tests.yml`). One of them checks the cards of a few hundred real lots from all the covered states (`tests/fixtures/`, captured on GitHub by `.github/workflows/capture.yml`); see `tests/README.md`.
